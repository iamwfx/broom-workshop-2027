How Neighborhoods Change: Housing Policy, Segregation, and New Approaches

A one-day workshop on how policy drives neighborhood change, and how those changes sustain or erode segregation and urban inequality.

Call for papers

Population change at the neighborhood level is rarely spontaneous. Cycles of investment and disinvestment, often driven by government intervention, shape who is able to arrive and who is forced to leave a neighborhood. The policies governing land use, subsidies, siting, and access to credit are among the strongest levers that determine neighborhood change and individual outcomes.

This workshop convenes researchers across demography, urban planning, geography, and sociology to ask how policy drives neighborhood change, and how these changes sustain or erode segregation and urban inequality. We will also examine how novel administrative, historical, and commercial data sources can shed new, measurable light on these processes.

We invite work in progress on topics including:

  • Zoning and land-use reform, such as upzoning, and who arrives and who is priced out as a result
  • Housing affordability, including rent burdens, rent regulation and tenant protections, inclusionary zoning, and how affordability pressures sort households across neighborhoods and regions
  • The importance of place in housing policy: where subsidized housing is sited, how “opportunity neighborhoods” are defined and targeted, and what this means for who lives where
  • Access to credit, from redlining to contemporary mortgage lending, and long-run neighborhood trajectories
  • Public investment and disinvestment, including transit-oriented development, urban renewal, and other place-based programs, and their role in displacement
  • Climate risk, including disasters, flood exposure, and rising insurance costs, as a driver of who leaves and who arrives
  • Investors and the financialization of housing, including institutional buyers of single-family homes, short-term rentals, and shifts in rental ownership
  • Measuring moves and neighborhood dynamics with administrative, historical, and commercial data, and how to assess their coverage and bias

Work in progress is welcome. If your submission is accepted, you will be asked to upload the full paper in advance of the workshop.

How to submit

Send an extended abstract of no more than one page. Submissions are open to faculty and graduate students. If you want to be considered only for a lightning talk, say so in the final comments question of the form.
